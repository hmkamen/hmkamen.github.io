Hannah Kamen, Ph.D.

Applied Economist · Environmental, Urban & Housing Economics

I study how climate risk and local land use regulation shape urban housing markets, and who gains or loses from them. My work pairs quasi-experimental methods with structural equilibrium models and high-resolution spatial data. I use it to ask practical policy questions: what households will pay for cooler neighborhoods, whether public heat-mitigation investment reaches the low-income residents it targets, and how zoning reform changes that answer.

I earned my Ph.D. in Mineral & Energy Economics (Applied Economics) from the Colorado School of Mines, advised by Jared Carbone. Before and during graduate school I worked as a data science team lead, as an economic consultant, and as a researcher at NREL, USDA ERS, and Resources for the Future.

Job Market Paper

Can Inclusionary Zoning Mitigate Climate Gentrification? Income-Based Sorting on Urban Cooling Investments

Draft available: PDF

  • Phoenix's Cool Corridors program alone: low-income renters keep $20 of the $432 the cooling is worth to them once rents adjust.
  • Paired with targeted minimum-lot reform: the low-income gain rises to $506 per year.
  • The ratio of high- to low-income gains falls from 27 to 1.
Abstract

Cities investing to reduce urban heat face a climate gentrification dilemma. Cooling raises neighborhood amenity values, but the rent increases that follow fall hardest on the low-income residents that investments aim to help. Using a non-homothetic spatial equilibrium model of household sorting in Maricopa County, Arizona, I study whether residential zoning reform can moderate this regressive incidence. On its own, Phoenix's Cool Corridors program provides negligible returns to low-income renters, who keep only $20 of the $432 the cooling is worth to them once rents adjust. Combining cooling with minimum-lot reform targeted to the most constrained neighborhoods reverses this effect. Under the combined policy, the low-income gain rises to $506 a year, the ratio of high- to low-income gains falls from twenty-seven to one, and more low-income residents remain in improved neighborhoods. I also show that homothetic demand, conventional in the environmental sorting literature, understates the affordability effects of neighborhood improvement, with consequences for policy conclusions.

Working Papers

The Price of Cool: How Urban Heat Shapes Neighborhood Value

Abstract

How far does the value households place on public cooling infrastructure, such as shade trees, extend across space? I develop a novel instrumental variable based on fine-scale urban surface airflow to isolate exogenous variation in neighborhood temperatures. I use it to estimate a hedonic model that recovers willingness to pay for cooling at multiple spatial scales, drawing on residential sales data from Maricopa County, AZ and 1km-resolution NCAR urban air temperature data. Households pay $676 annually for a 1°C reduction in summer temperatures within a few blocks of the home and $245 for equivalent cooling at the broader neighborhood scale. Phoenix's Cool Corridors program generates $137 million in benefits, and evaluations confined to the corridor's census tract understate that value by 53%.

Extreme Heat and U.S. Labor Relocation: Implications for Inequality

Abstract

I examine the wage impacts of climate-induced migration across the United States by educational attainment. I link climate preferences recovered from a discrete model of location choice to a large-scale computable general equilibrium model of the U.S. economy that endogenizes wages and housing prices. Climate-induced migration moves real wages between +2.1% and −1.7% for college-educated workers and between +1.5% and −1.4% for high-school-equivalent workers across states. The real wage gap between the two groups widens most in the southern U.S., by 1.4 to 3.5 percent, where projected extreme heat concentrates and the difference in migratory response between skilled and unskilled workers is largest.
