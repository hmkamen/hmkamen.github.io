About

Hello and thanks for visiting my website! I am an applied environmental and resource economist. My research investigates how environmental risk and local land use regulation reshape urban housing markets and the welfare of the households who live in them.

I successfully defended my Ph.D. in Mineral & Energy Economics (Applied Economics) at the Colorado School of Mines in August 2026 and will graduate in December 2026. I also hold an M.S. in Mineral & Energy Economics from the Colorado School of Mines and a B.A. in Economics from the University of Rochester. Before and during graduate school I worked as a data scientist, an economic consultant, and a researcher at NREL, the USDA Economic Research Service, and Resources for the Future.

Research interests: Environmental & Resource Economics · Energy Economics · Urban Economics · Housing & Land Use Policy · Climate Adaptation · Applied Econometrics & Causal Inference · Structural & Spatial Equilibrium Modeling

Job Market Paper

Can Inclusionary Zoning Mitigate Climate Gentrification? Income-Based Sorting on Urban Cooling Investments PDF ↗

Methods: structural modeling · discrete choice · instrumental variables · policy analysis · spatial data & GIS

Using a spatial equilibrium model of household sorting in Maricopa County, AZ, I show that public cooling investments (e.g., shade trees, cool pavement) can raise rents and displace the low-income residents they aim to help. I find that Phoenix's Cool Corridors program on its own leaves low-income renters with only $20 of the $432 the cooling is worth to them. However, pairing it with minimum-lot residential zoning reform targeted to the most constrained neighborhoods raises their gain to $506 a year.

The Price of Cool: How Urban Heat Shapes Neighborhood Value

Revisions requested, Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists

Methods: causal inference · instrumental variables · difference-in-differences · hedonic valuation · spatial econometrics

Abstract

I develop a novel instrumental variable based on urban surface airflow to estimate what households are willing to pay for public cooling (e.g. shade trees, cool pavement) at multiple spatial scales. I find that households are willing to pay $676 a year for a 1°C reduction in summer temperatures near the home and $245 for equivalent neighborhood-wide cooling. Furthermore, I show that because urban cooling benefits spill over beyond the corridor's census tract, tract-level evaluations understate the $137 million value of Phoenix's Cool Corridors program by 53%.

Extreme Heat and U.S. Labor Relocation: Implications for Inequality

Methods: structural modeling · discrete choice · computable general equilibrium (CGE)

Abstract

Using a discrete choice model of household location linked to a computable general equilibrium model of the U.S. economy, I project how climate-induced migration affects wages by educational attainment. I find that college-educated workers leave the South at higher rates than non-college-educated workers. Less-educated workers who remain in southern states see their wages fall by up to 1.4%, while remaining college-educated workers' wages increase by up to 2.1%.
